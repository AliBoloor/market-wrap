## Executive thesis

September exposed the difference between Canada's **second-quarter rebound** and its **third-quarter momentum**. The Bank of Canada summarized second-quarter growth at 3.3% annualized, but Statistics Canada's newly released industry measure showed real GDP essentially unchanged in July. August then brought a 42,000 decline in employment, while headline CPI stayed at 3.0%. The economy is not in a generalized contraction: construction expanded, second-quarter productivity rose, and oil-and-gas investment strengthened. But demand, hiring, manufacturing, trade and financial conditions no longer point in the same direction. ([Bank of Canada September decision](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/2026/09/fad-press-release-2026-09-02/); [Statistics Canada July GDP](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/daily-quotidien/260929/dq260929a-eng.htm); [Statistics Canada August labour force](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/daily-quotidien/260904/dq260904a-eng.htm))

The month's central policy problem is therefore a two-sided one. High energy prices, a weaker Canadian dollar and new tariffs can lift costs, while weak labour demand and trade uncertainty can restrain growth. The Bank held its policy rate at 2.25%, but the two-year Government of Canada yield rose 36 basis points during the month and the 10-year yield rose 23 basis points through the latest available official observation. Fiscal policy moved toward investment incentives and tariff support, increasing the importance of transparent costing and measurable additional investment.

**Interpretation:** Canada enters October with positive structural opportunities—resources, a productivity rebound, population growth, and a potentially powerful investment incentive—but a fragile cyclical handoff. Policy success should be judged by whether private investment, productivity and export diversification rise without turning temporary tariff protection or broad tax relief into permanent, poorly targeted fiscal costs.

## Growth and demand

Real GDP by industry was essentially unchanged in July after the second-quarter rebound. Construction grew 1.3%, its fourth consecutive increase, and utilities rose 1.7%; those gains were offset by weakness in manufacturing (-0.9%), mining, quarrying and oil and gas extraction (-0.5%), and retail trade. Ten of 20 industrial sectors expanded. Statistics Canada revised monthly history back to January 2025, so the zero reading should be treated as the latest estimate rather than an immutable final number. ([Statistics Canada July GDP](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/daily-quotidien/260929/dq260929a-eng.htm))

Household demand also softened at the start of the quarter. Retail sales fell 0.7% in July to C$73.7 billion; Statistics Canada's advance estimate suggested a 1.3% rebound in August, but that preliminary estimate is subject to revision. ([Statistics Canada July retail trade](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/daily-quotidien/260924/dq260924a-eng.htm?indgeo=13&indid=3660-1))

The stronger structural signal came from productivity. Business-sector labour productivity rose 1.0% in the second quarter, the largest increase since the pandemic-distorted second quarter of 2020, with gains in 12 of 16 major industries. Yet hours worked fell 0.1% and unit labour costs rose 0.6%. A single strong quarter is encouraging, not proof that Canada's long-running productivity weakness is solved. ([Statistics Canada productivity](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/daily-quotidien/260903/dq260903c-eng.htm))

## Households, housing, labour and population

Employment fell **42,000 (-0.2%)** in August, the employment rate slipped to 60.8%, and unemployment held at 6.4%. Manufacturing added 22,000 jobs, but natural resources, utilities, public administration and business/building support services lost jobs. Quebec employment fell 19,000 and Ontario employment fell 18,000. Average hourly wages were up 2.0% year over year, slowing from 2.8% in July. The combination of slower wages and a 3.0% CPI implies little aggregate real-wage growth in this one-month comparison, though household outcomes differ widely and monthly labour estimates are noisy. ([Statistics Canada August labour force](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/daily-quotidien/260904/dq260904a-eng.htm))

Household balance sheets improved at the margin but remain leveraged. In the second quarter, household credit-market debt fell to 176.4% of disposable income from 178.6%, and the debt-service ratio declined to 14.52%. Mortgage interest payments still rose 1.6%, their largest increase in two years, while mortgage borrowing slowed to its lowest pace since early 2024. Household net worth rose 2.9%, driven partly by financial markets, but Statistics Canada notes that 69.0% of financial assets were held by the highest wealth quintile in its latest distributional data. ([Statistics Canada national balance sheet](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/daily-quotidien/260911/dq260911a-eng.htm))

Housing supply was not accelerating. Canada's six-month housing-starts trend fell 1.3% in August to 244,149 annualized units; the standalone monthly SAAR was roughly flat at 229,046. Year-to-date actual starts in centres of at least 10,000 people were down 4% from 2025, while completions fell 11.2% from July. Quebec and Alberta improved modestly, but Ontario weakened. This is an affordability warning: slower population growth can ease demand pressure, but supply and completion capacity still matter. ([CMHC August housing starts](https://www.cmhc-schl.gc.ca/media-newsroom/news-releases/2026/housing-starts-construction-data-august-2026))

Canada's population reached 41.80 million on July 1, up 0.2% in the second quarter. The estimated non-permanent-resident population declined 0.7% to 2.78 million, while immigrant arrivals of 99,148 were 4.2% below a year earlier. Slower demographic growth can reduce near-term pressure on housing and services, but it also slows labour-force and aggregate-demand growth; productivity and labour-force participation therefore matter more for potential growth. ([Statistics Canada population estimates](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/daily-quotidien/260923/dq260923b-eng.htm))

## Inflation, rates and financial conditions

August CPI inflation held at **3.0% year over year**. Gasoline rose 22.8%, transportation 7.5%, food bought from stores 2.8%, and shelter 1.5%. CPI excluding gasoline accelerated to 2.4% from 2.2%; the monthly headline index fell 0.1%, while the seasonally adjusted index rose 0.2%. The correct conclusion is neither that inflation is entirely energy-driven nor that broad inflation has reaccelerated decisively: energy dominated the headline, but the ex-gas measure and rent warrant monitoring. ([Statistics Canada August CPI](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/daily-quotidien/260914/dq260914a-eng.htm))

The Bank of Canada kept the overnight target at **2.25%** on September 2. It judged the second-quarter recovery broad but noted excess supply, tariff uncertainty and growing upside inflation risks from energy and import costs. The next decision and Monetary Policy Report are due October 28. ([Bank of Canada September decision](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/2026/09/fad-press-release-2026-09-02/); [September Governing Council deliberations](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/2026/09/summary-of-governing-council-deliberations-fixed-announcement-date-of-september-2-2026/?trk=article-ssr-frontend-pulse_little-text-block))

Market rates tightened despite the unchanged policy rate. The benchmark two-year yield rose from 3.01% on August 31 to 3.37% on September 28, while the 10-year rose from 3.73% to 3.96%. At generation time, the official yield lookup stopped at September 28; September 30 was a banking/non-settlement holiday. The Bank's daily USD/CAD average rose from 1.3866 on August 31 to 1.4188 on September 29, a 2.32% rise in CAD per U.S. dollar and therefore a weaker Canadian dollar. ([Bank of Canada bond-yield lookup](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/rates/interest-rates/lookup-bond-yields/); [Bank of Canada USD/CAD observations](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/valet/observations/FXUSDCAD/json?start_date=2026-08-31&end_date=2026-09-30))

## Fiscal policy, trade, resources and industry

July merchandise exports fell 2.3% and imports rose 2.2%, narrowing the goods surplus to C$769 million from C$4.2 billion. The bilateral surplus with the United States fell to C$5.9 billion, its lowest since February. Diversification was visible: exports to non-U.S. markets rose 7.4% to a record C$25.6 billion, led by shipments to the Netherlands, China and Germany, but one month does not establish a durable reorientation. ([Statistics Canada July merchandise trade](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/daily-quotidien/260903/dq260903a-eng.htm))

Canada's counter-tariffs of 15%, 25% and 50% on selected U.S. goods took effect September 8 after the United States imposed 50% tariffs on C$27.6 billion of Canadian goods. Later U.S. restrictions targeted selected Canadian dairy, alcohol and motorcycle imports and federal procurement access. The direct incidence is concentrated, but the larger macro channel is uncertainty: delayed investment, impaired supply chains and higher intermediate-goods costs. ([Finance Canada tariff list](https://www.canada.ca/en/department-finance/programs/international-trade-finance-policy/canadas-response-us-tariffs/complete-list-us-products-subject-to-counter-tariffs.html); [Associated Press, September 10](https://apnews.com/article/700d1eaad7f1aa87f1d7875222691b35); [Associated Press, September 29](https://apnews.com/article/bdb4d9b946e98f84088c5c0f7f522588))

The federal government proposed a permanent **Productivity Mega Deduction** that would allow immediate expensing for roughly two-thirds of eligible capital investment. Finance Canada estimates an incremental fiscal cost of C$36 billion over five years and says the measure would reduce Canada's marginal effective tax rate on new investment from roughly 13.0% to 6.4%. Those are government estimates, not observed outcomes. The policy lowers the tax cost of capital; its test is whether it creates additional Canadian investment rather than accelerating projects that would have occurred anyway. ([Finance Canada backgrounder](https://www.canada.ca/en/department-finance/news/2026/09/government-of-canada-introduces-new-productivity-mega-deduction-to-boost-canadas-advantage-as-the-most-competitive-g7-country-for-new-business-inve.html))

Independent fiscal scrutiny remains important. The Parliamentary Budget Officer found that classifications under the government's operating-budget anchor sometimes appear contradictory and would benefit from a formal methodology. Meanwhile, oil-and-gas capital spending rose 7.2% in the second quarter to C$11.0 billion, illustrating how resource income can support investment and provincial revenues even as higher oil prices squeeze consumers and non-energy firms. ([PBO operating-budget anchor](https://fallback--repli.pbo-dpb.ca/en/publications/RP-2627-010-S--government-operating-budget-fiscal-anchor--cible-budget-fonctionnement-gouvernement); [Statistics Canada oil-and-gas capital spending](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/daily-quotidien/260901/dq260901a-eng.htm))

## Politics and policy

The governing policy mix now combines retaliation, sector support, trade diversification, major-project promotion and a broad investment tax incentive. These tools answer different problems. Retaliation seeks bargaining leverage; worker and firm support manages adjustment; infrastructure and trade reform address capacity; tax incentives target private investment. Conflating them makes accountability harder. Each should have transparent objectives, costs, review dates and distributional analysis.

Competing views are substantive. Market-oriented analysts emphasize taxes, regulatory certainty, ports and competition. Progressive analysts favor public coordination, worker protections, domestic procurement and conditions on public support. Centrist institutional analysis stresses broad tax reform, internal-market barriers and trade diversification. The useful common ground is that Canada needs more productive investment and less vulnerability to one external market; disagreement concerns who directs investment, who bears risk and how benefits are shared.

Provincial effects are uneven. TD Economics forecasts stronger 2026 growth in resource-intensive provinces and weaker growth in Ontario and Quebec as tariffs and export restrictions widen regional gaps; that is a private forecast, not an observed fact. The October 5 Quebec election is the nearest political milestone with potential implications for provincial fiscal, housing, energy and industrial policy. ([TD provincial forecast](https://economics.td.com/provincial-economic-forecast); [Élections Québec](https://www.quebec.ca/en/news/actualites/detail/2026-provincial-election-polling-day-is-october-5-72332))

## Outlook and scenarios

October's sequence is unusually informative. Statistics Canada is scheduled to release August trade on **October 6**, September labour data on **October 9**, September CPI on **October 19**, and August GDP by industry on **October 30**. The Bank releases its Business Outlook Survey and Canadian Survey of Consumer Expectations on **October 19**, followed by the policy decision and Monetary Policy Report on **October 28**. Budget 2026 is due this fall, but no exact tabling date was confirmed at publication time. ([Statistics Canada release calendar](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/en/release-diffusion/2026-eng.pdf?st=JwXqPj0_); [Bank of Canada 2026 schedule](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/2025/08/bank-canada-publishes-2026-schedule-policy-interest-rate-announcements-other-major-publications/); [Finance Canada budget consultations](https://www.canada.ca/en/department-finance/news/2026/07/government-of-canada-launches-consultations-ahead-of-budget-2026.html))

- **Base case:** growth remains modest after the second-quarter rebound; trade-sensitive manufacturing and hiring weaken, while construction and resource income prevent a broad contraction. Headline inflation stays energy-sensitive and the Bank holds at 2.25%.
- **Upside case:** non-U.S. exports broaden, the August retail rebound is confirmed, business surveys show the tax incentive and major-project pipeline are unlocking investment, and tariff escalation pauses. Productivity gains persist without a renewed inflation impulse.
- **Downside case:** tariffs and import restrictions expand, employment losses spread beyond directly exposed industries, the weaker currency and energy costs lift non-gas inflation, and higher bond yields deepen housing and debt-service stress. The Bank then faces weaker growth and higher inflation simultaneously.

Key signposts are the breadth of job losses, real rather than nominal consumer spending, CPI excluding gasoline, new capital orders, non-U.S. export volumes, mortgage arrears and renewals, the two-year yield, and whether announced investment incentives generate projects rather than press releases.
