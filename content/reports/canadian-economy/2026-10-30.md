## Executive thesis

Canada ended October with a sharper policy conflict than it began with. Real GDP by industry was essentially unchanged in both July and August, Statistics Canada's advance estimate points to a 0.1% decline in September, and employment fell by 68,000 in September after a 42,000 decline in August. Yet headline CPI rose 3.1% year over year, largely because gasoline was 27.8% more expensive. The Bank of Canada responded by raising the overnight rate 25 basis points to **2.50%**, its first increase since July 2023. ([Statistics Canada August GDP](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/daily-quotidien/261030/dq261030a-eng.htm); [Statistics Canada September labour force](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/daily-quotidien/261009/dq261009a-eng.htm); [Statistics Canada September CPI](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/daily-quotidien/261019/dq261019a-eng.htm?indgeo=0&indid=3665-2); [Bank of Canada October decision](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/2026/10/fad-press-release-2026-10-28/))

The contradiction is real, not statistical noise: domestic demand and trade retained pockets of strength, while labour slack and tariff-sensitive industries weakened; energy lifted national income and the TSX, while also raising household costs and the risk of wider price pass-through. The Bank's October forecast expects inflation near or above 3% through much of 2027, but its own decision record says core measures remain near 2% and excess supply persists. ([Bank of Canada October Monetary Policy Report](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/publications/mpr/mpr-2026-10-28/))

**Interpretation:** the next phase is a test of policy targeting. Monetary policy must prevent an energy shock from becoming generalized without treating weak hiring as irrelevant. Fiscal and industrial policy must distinguish temporary tariff adjustment from permanent subsidy, and investment incentives must be judged by additional productive capacity rather than announced dollars. The November 5 federal budget will be the first major test of that balance.

## Growth, demand, productivity and population

Real GDP by industry was unchanged in August, the second flat month. Services edged down 0.1% and goods edged up 0.1%. Retail trade activity rose 1.2%, manufacturing gained 0.3% and construction expanded, but wholesale trade fell 1.4%, public administration fell as census work wound down, and oil and gas extraction declined 1.2% during maintenance. Statistics Canada's preliminary September estimate is -0.1%; on that incomplete monthly-industry basis, third-quarter growth was about 0.3%. The official expenditure-based third-quarter estimate is not due until November 30. ([Statistics Canada August GDP](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/daily-quotidien/261030/dq261030a-eng.htm))

Household spending was firmer than the output headline. August retail sales rose 1.2% to C$74.6 billion, with all nine subsectors higher; volumes also rose 1.2%. Core retail sales excluding autos and fuel rose 1.1%. The advance September estimate of +0.7% is based on a 58.5% response rate and is explicitly preliminary. ([Statistics Canada August retail trade](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/daily-quotidien/261023/dq261023a-eng.htm))

The latest official productivity estimate remains encouraging but incomplete: business-sector labour productivity rose 1.0% in the second quarter, while hours worked fell 0.1% and unit labour costs rose 0.6%. October supplied no newer quarterly productivity observation. The Bank attributes part of the mid-year rebound to oil-and-gas and machinery investment; whether the federal Productivity Mega Deduction produces additional investment rather than timing shifts is still unobserved. ([Statistics Canada second-quarter productivity](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/daily-quotidien/260903/dq260903c-eng.htm))

Population growth is slowing as the estimated non-permanent-resident population declines. Canada's population was 41.80 million on July 1, up 0.2% in the second quarter; the latest release estimated 2.78 million non-permanent residents, down 0.7% in the quarter. Slower population growth reduces near-term pressure on aggregate demand and housing, but also makes productivity and participation more important to potential growth. ([Statistics Canada population estimates](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/daily-quotidien/260923/dq260923b-eng.htm))

## Households, housing and labour

Employment declined **68,000 (-0.3%)** in September, split between full- and part-time work, after a 42,000 decline in August. The unemployment rate rose to 6.5%, the employment rate fell to 60.6%, and participation fell to 64.8%—the lowest since December 1997 outside the pandemic. Statistics Canada attributes much of the longer-run participation decline to population aging, but the month's weakness was also cyclical: the job-finding rate was below both a year earlier and its 2017-19 average. Average hourly wages rose 2.3% year over year, below the 3.1% headline CPI in this rough aggregate comparison. ([Statistics Canada September labour force](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/daily-quotidien/261009/dq261009a-eng.htm))

The regional split widened. Quebec lost 49,000 jobs and British Columbia 20,000; Alberta added 23,000. Quebec employment was down 130,000 from January, while Alberta employment was 2.9% above a year earlier. Manufacturing employment fell 13,000 nationally, a material warning given the tariff shock, although monthly Labour Force Survey estimates carry sampling variability.

Housing construction was steady at a level that still trails last year. CMHC's six-month starts trend rose 0.6% to 250,411 annualized units in September, and the standalone monthly SAAR rose 3% to 248,896. Actual starts in centres of at least 10,000 people were down 11% year over year in September and 4% year to date. Montréal's trend fell 8%, Vancouver's rose 6%, and Toronto was flat. ([CMHC September housing starts](https://www.cmhc-schl.gc.ca/professionals/housing-markets-data-and-research/housing-data/data-tables/housing-market-data/monthly-housing-starts-construction-data-tables))

Mortgage stress is rising from a low base. CMHC's fall report put the mortgage-arrears rate at 0.25%, up three basis points from a year earlier; the average mortgage payment was C$1,883 in the first quarter, 3.92% above a year earlier, and total mortgage debt outstanding reached C$2.447 trillion in July. Mortgage-investment entities had much higher arrears than banks or credit unions, so the aggregate conceals lender and borrower differences. ([CMHC Residential Mortgage Industry Report](https://www.cmhc-schl.gc.ca/professionals/housing-markets-data-and-research/housing-research/research-reports/housing-finance/residential-mortgage-industry-report))

## Inflation, rates and financial conditions

September CPI inflation rose to **3.1%** from 3.0%. Gasoline increased 27.8% year over year and energy 19.0%; CPI excluding gasoline slowed to 2.2% from 2.4%. Grocery inflation eased to 1.7%, shelter inflation rose to 1.8%, and rent increased 2.8%. The seasonally adjusted monthly CPI rose 0.4%. This is still an energy-led shock, but its duration raises the risk that transportation and production costs spread into broader prices. ([Statistics Canada September CPI](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/daily-quotidien/261019/dq261019a-eng.htm?indgeo=0&indid=3665-2))

The Bank of Canada raised the overnight target to 2.50%, the Bank Rate to 2.75% and the deposit rate to 2.45%. Its forecast has 2026 real GDP growth at 1%, followed by 2% in 2027 and 2028, while CPI reaches about 3.5% by year-end and averages 3% in 2027. The Bank acknowledged the alternative case: core inflation remains close to target, economic slack remains, and new U.S. tariffs will weigh on growth. Its decision is therefore risk management against future pass-through, not a claim that inflation is already broad. ([Bank of Canada October decision](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/2026/10/fad-press-release-2026-10-28/); [Bank of Canada October Monetary Policy Report](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/publications/mpr/mpr-2026-10-28/))

Official benchmark yields moved differently across the curve. From October 1 to the latest October 29 observation, the two-year yield rose from 3.27% to 3.36%, while the 10-year fell from 3.94% to 3.84%. The positive 10s-minus-2s spread narrowed from 67 to 48 basis points. ([Bank of Canada Valet: two- and ten-year benchmark yields](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/valet/observations/BD.CDN.2YR.DQ.YLD,BD.CDN.10YR.DQ.YLD/json?start_date=2026-09-30&end_date=2026-10-30))

The official daily-average USD/CAD rate fell from 1.4188 on September 29 to 1.3960 on October 30, meaning fewer Canadian dollars per U.S. dollar and a 1.61% decline in USD/CAD. That currency improvement cushions import costs at the margin but does not erase the oil and tariff shock. ([Bank of Canada Valet: USD/CAD](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/valet/observations/FXUSDCAD/json?start_date=2026-09-30&end_date=2026-10-30))

## Fiscal policy, trade, resources and industry

August merchandise exports rose 2.5% and imports fell 2.0%, widening the surplus to C$4.2 billion. Exports to the United States rose 8.1% ahead of new tariffs, and the bilateral surplus widened to C$11.2 billion; exports to non-U.S. markets fell 8.5% after July's record. The timing suggests front-loading is plausible, so the result should not be read as proof that the tariff shock improved sustainable trade performance. ([Statistics Canada August merchandise trade](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/daily-quotidien/261006/dq261006a-eng.htm))

The Parliamentary Budget Officer projects real GDP growth of 0.9% in 2026 and 1.5% in 2027 under tariffs in force on October 1. It estimates cumulative deficits of C$333.3 billion over five years, federal borrowing of C$732.9 billion and a debt-to-GDP ratio rising from 40.8% in 2026-27 to 42.6% in 2030-31. The PBO expects only the operating-balance anchor to be met and says the Productivity Mega Deduction is the largest new fiscal measure since June. These are independent baseline projections, not final Budget 2026 numbers. ([PBO Economic and Fiscal Outlook](https://www.pbo-dpb.ca/en/publications/RP-2627-011-S--economic-fiscal-outlook-october-2026--perspectives-economiques-financieres-octobre-2026))

Finance Canada will table Budget 2026 on **November 5**. The announced agenda emphasizes investment, strategic projects, affordability and economic security; the budget will determine whether new measures improve the debt path and whether capital spending has transparent selection, costing and performance tests. ([Finance Canada Budget 2026 advisory](https://www.canada.ca/en/department-finance/news/2026/10/government-of-canada-to-release-budget-2026-on-november-5-2026.html))

Federal and Ontario governments also announced an C$8.7 billion tariff-response and industrial plan, including steel, auto and critical-mineral support. The economic case is strongest where temporary support preserves viable capacity or unlocks productive investment; the risks are weak additionality, incumbent protection and long-lived commitments without exit tests. ([Canada-Ontario action plan](https://www.pm.gc.ca/en/news/news-releases/2026/10/21/prime-minister-carney-and-premier-ford-launch-new-action-plan-protect))

Resources remained Canada's buffer and inflation hazard. WTI futures rose 13.72% in October to US$102.83 per barrel. August oil-and-gas output fell during maintenance, while potash mining rebounded 12.0%. Higher resource prices support producers, nominal GDP and some provincial revenues, but raise transport and household costs. Canada's tariff-remission framework can reduce harm where affected inputs cannot reasonably be sourced domestically or from non-U.S. suppliers, but relief is administrative and case-specific. ([Finance Canada tariff-remission framework](https://www.canada.ca/en/department-finance/programs/international-trade-finance-policy/process-requesting-remission-tariffs-that-apply-on-certain-goods-us.html))

## Politics and policy

Quebec's October 5 election produced a minority legislature: the Parti québécois won 60 of 127 seats with 28.02% of the vote. The result matters economically because a minority government must negotiate fiscal, energy, housing and intergovernmental policy; it does not by itself establish which measures will pass. Quebec had already recorded the sharpest provincial employment decline from January through September, making near-term labour stabilization and longer-run productivity separate policy tests. ([Élections Québec results](https://www.electionsquebec.qc.ca/en/results-and-statistics/))

At the federal level, trade resilience, economic security and industrial capacity moved closer to the centre of policy. The central debate is not whether Canada should respond to the tariff shock, but how: direct subsidy and procurement can protect strategic capacity; tax and regulatory reform can improve investment incentives; worker-focused adjustment can spread transition costs. Each tool needs a stated objective, transparent cost, measurable result and sunset or review mechanism.

## Outlook and scenarios

November's sequence is unusually informative. Statistics Canada is scheduled to publish September merchandise trade on November 4, October labour data on November 6, October CPI on November 16, September retail trade on November 20, and third-quarter GDP plus September GDP by industry on November 30. Budget 2026 arrives November 5. The next Bank of Canada decision is December 9. ([Statistics Canada 2026-27 release dates](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/en/release-diffusion/2026-eng.pdf?st=JwXqPj0_); [Bank of Canada 2026 schedule](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/2025/08/bank-canada-publishes-2026-schedule-policy-interest-rate-announcements-other-major-publications/))

- **Base case:** energy keeps headline inflation elevated, but ex-gas and core measures remain near target; growth slows under tariffs without a broad recession. The Bank holds in December while assessing pass-through. Budget measures emphasize investment and adjustment, with only partial near-term demand support.
- **Upside case:** trade restrictions stabilize, employment stops falling, business investment responds to incentives, housing construction improves and the Canadian dollar limits imported inflation. Third-quarter GDP confirms that the summer rebound broadened.
- **Downside case:** October labour losses deepen, new tariffs weaken manufacturing and exports, oil remains high enough to lift costs but not enough to offset national demand weakness, and fiscal measures add borrowing without measurable productive capacity. The Bank then faces persistent headline inflation alongside a softer labour market.

Key signposts are employment breadth, CPI excluding gasoline, firms' reported price pass-through, September trade after tariff front-loading, the composition and costing of Budget 2026, the TSX 34,953 support area, and whether two-year yields remain elevated relative to the 2.50% policy rate.

## Data quality

- Official releases published in October describe different periods: August GDP, trade and retail; September labour and CPI; second-quarter productivity; and mortgage indicators with several reference dates. They are not one synchronous snapshot.
- September GDP by industry (-0.1%) and September retail sales (+0.7%) are advance estimates. Third-quarter expenditure-based GDP is not available until November 30.
- The Labour Force Survey is sample-based and monthly changes can be volatile. The wage/CPI comparison is an aggregate year-over-year snapshot, not a household-level real-income calculation.
- Official Bank of Canada yields stop at October 29; currency data extend through October 30. The prior currency comparison uses September 29 because September 30 was a banking holiday.
- Yahoo's CAD=X and TD histories failed strict OHLC validation and were excluded. Official Bank of Canada USD/CAD replaced the currency observation; no substitute TD series was fabricated.
- Market calculations use October 30 observations retrieved after the Canadian close. Futures are not spot commodities, and U.S.-listed company shares are not Toronto listings.
