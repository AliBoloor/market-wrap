## Executive thesis

August replaced Canada's recession concern with a more complicated problem. Second-quarter GDP rebounded **0.8% quarter over quarter (3.3% annualized)** and real GDP per capita rose 1.0%, while July employment increased by 75,000. At the same time, headline inflation returned to 3.0%, oil remained high, and the collapse of Canada–U.S. trade negotiations produced new 50% U.S. tariffs and announced Canadian countermeasures. The economy entered September with better domestic momentum but sharply worse policy uncertainty.

That mix argues for neither complacency nor a simple downturn story. Strong exports and investment demonstrate capacity to recover, but tariff exposure, weak long-run productivity, household leverage and energy-driven inflation leave the expansion unusually sensitive to policy. The TSX gained 2.96% in August, consistent with resilience, while individual bank, pipeline and railway returns diverged—evidence that the macro benefit of resources was not evenly shared.

## Growth and demand

Real GDP grew 0.8% in the second quarter after first-quarter growth was revised to 0.1%. Exports rose 3.6%, their fastest increase since early 2023, led by a 27% rebound in passenger-car and light-truck exports. Household spending and business capital investment also contributed. Monthly GDP by industry rose 0.3% in June, with 13 of 20 sectors expanding. ([Statistics Canada quarterly GDP](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/daily-quotidien/260828/dq260828a-eng.htm); [Statistics Canada GDP by industry](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/daily-quotidien/260828/dq260828b-eng.htm))

The composition matters. Stronger exports and machinery investment are healthier than inventory-led growth, but auto production was rebounding from prior disruptions and the new tariff shock arrived after the quarter ended. The household saving rate was only 3.7%, limiting the cushion against higher prices or renewed job weakness. Canada's medium-term test is whether the rebound becomes productivity-enhancing private investment rather than a one-quarter normalization.

## Households, housing and labor

Employment increased **75,000 (+0.4%)** in July, the unemployment rate edged down to 6.4%, and gains were concentrated in private-sector employment and self-employment. Average hourly wages rose 2.8% year over year, slower than June. That is a welcome improvement, but unemployment remained elevated and the report predated the late-August tariff escalation. ([Statistics Canada labour force survey](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/daily-quotidien/260807/dq260807a-eng.htm))

Housing remained a structural affordability and productivity challenge. Lower shelter inflation—1.3% year over year in July—helped the CPI arithmetic, but does not by itself repair high price-to-income ratios, mortgage-reset exposure, or the construction shortfall. Policy that improves permitting, infrastructure and construction productivity has a better chance of expanding supply than demand subsidies alone.

## Inflation, rates and financial conditions

July CPI accelerated to **3.0% year over year** from 2.8%. Gasoline and travel tours drove much of the increase; CPI excluding gasoline remained 2.2%, CPI-trim was 1.9%, and CPI-median 2.0%. This is a crucial distinction: underlying inflation was near target, but households still experienced the full energy shock. ([Statistics Canada CPI](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/daily-quotidien/260817/dq260817a-eng.htm); [Bank of Canada key variables](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/rates/indicators/key-variables/))

The Bank of Canada held its policy rate at **2.25%** throughout August. At month-end the benchmark two-year Government of Canada yield was 3.01% and the 10-year yield 3.73%, up from 3.27% for the 10-year on July 31. The curve priced materially tighter future conditions than the overnight target and reflected both global term-premium and domestic inflation risk. ([Bank of Canada policy rate](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/core-functions/monetary-policy/key-interest-rate/); [Bank of Canada bond yields](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/rates/interest-rates/canadian-bonds/))

## Fiscal policy, trade, resources and industry

The defining August policy event was the collapse of trade talks. The United States imposed 50% tariffs on C$27.6 billion of Canadian goods effective August 22. Ottawa announced matching tariffs at rates of 15%, 25% and 50% beginning September 8 and a **C$7.5 billion** support package, including regional liquidity and BDC programs. ([Finance Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/department-finance/news/2026/08/canada-announces-targeted-countermeasures-and-substantive-support-for-workers-and-businesses-in-response-to-us-tariffs.html))

Retaliation may protect bargaining credibility and selected producers, but it also raises input and consumer costs. Support can prevent temporary liquidity problems from destroying viable capacity; if maintained too broadly or too long, it can preserve low-productivity structures. The strongest durable response is diversification plus domestic reform: interprovincial trade, infrastructure, permitting, competition and investment incentives.

Energy is both buffer and hazard. WTI futures ended August at $85.76, up 1.29% for the month after a much wider $74.24–$89.00 range. Higher oil supports producers, exports and some provincial revenues, but raises transportation and manufacturing costs and complicates the Bank's inflation task.

## Politics and policy

Prime Minister Mark Carney's government moved from negotiation to a dollar-for-dollar response, while emphasizing worker support, strategic sectors, export diversification and a “one Canadian economy” agenda. That position has broad political appeal after the U.S. escalation, but the economic trade-offs remain contested. Some provincial voices favor a more selective response to reduce domestic damage; progressive analysts favor more active public ownership and managed trade; market-oriented analysts emphasize productivity, competition and tax reform.

Those positions answer different questions. Retaliation concerns leverage and fairness; adjustment assistance concerns transition costs; productivity reform concerns long-run living standards. A credible policy package needs all three horizons, with transparent costs and sunset conditions rather than treating any one instrument as sufficient. ([Prime Minister's Office](https://www.pm.gc.ca/en/news/readouts/2026/08/22/prime-minister-carney-chairs-first-ministers-meeting); [Associated Press](https://apnews.com/article/4d18583fe52134ca8550652ad9772d2c))

## Outlook and scenarios

The next month will test whether domestic strength can absorb the trade shock. August labor data, new tariff implementation and incoming inflation will determine whether the Bank of Canada can remain at 2.25% without either reigniting inflation or allowing uncertainty to stall investment.

- **Base:** growth slows from the second-quarter rebound but remains positive; core inflation stays near 2%, while headline inflation remains energy-sensitive. The Bank holds.
- **Upside:** tariff exemptions or renewed talks limit supply-chain damage, investment persists, and oil income supports national demand without broader inflation pass-through.
- **Downside:** retaliation broadens, firms freeze capital spending and hiring, and elevated energy costs spread into core prices. The Bank then faces a growth-inflation trade-off rather than a straightforward easing response.

Watch the breadth of employment, business investment intentions, CPI excluding gasoline, the TSX 35,423 support area, and the two-year yield relative to the policy rate.
